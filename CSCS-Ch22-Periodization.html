Topic 01 — Central Concepts

Periodization vs Programming

Periodization
Macro-management of the training process.
Annual / multiyear direction + goals; scaffold for programming.
Programming
Micro-management: modes, methods, loads, sets/reps.
Fine-tuned by monitoring + athlete state.
Periodization = logical, systematic sequencing + integration of training → raises odds of peak performance at the right time.

Topic 01 — Central Concepts · 1 of 3

General Adaptation Syndrome

What It Is
Selye (1930s): 3-stage response to stress — alarm, resistance, exhaustion.
Foundation concept for periodization theory.
How It Works
Alarm → ↑ fatigue/soreness, ↓ performance.
Resistance → adapts, back to baseline → supercompensation if stress is structured + not excessive.
Training Implication
Stress persists → exhaustion = overreaching/overtraining → avoid via planning + monitoring.
Psychoemotional stress ↑ alarm fatigue, lengthens resistance → delayed supercompensation.
Topic 01 — Central Concepts · 2 of 3

Stimulus-Fatigue-Recovery-Adaptation

What It Is
Extension of the GAS: stimulus → fatigue → recovery → adaptation.
Bigger workload → more fatigue → longer delay to full recovery.
How It Works
Fatigue dissipates → preparedness ↑. No new stimulus → involution (detraining below baseline). New stimulus → cycle repeats.
Applies to exercise, session, day or cycle.
Training Implication
Full recovery isn't always needed before the next session → light/heavy sessions modulate fatigue.
Foundation for sequential models.
Topic 01 — Central Concepts · 3 of 3

Fitness-Fatigue Paradigm

What It Is
Zatsiorsky's two-factor model: every bout/session/cycle creates fitness + fatigue aftereffects.
Sum = preparedness.
How It Works
High load → fitness ↑ but fatigue ↑↑ → preparedness ↓. Low load → little fatigue, little fitness.
Fatigue dissipates faster than fitness → preparedness ↑ if fitness is retained.
Training Implication
Sequencing of load matters. Reduce too much → fatigue ↓ but fitness ↓ too.
Residual training effects → basis for sequential models.

Three Basic Models

Topic 02 — Planning the Training Process · 1 of 3

Parallel Models

What It Is
All training factors targeted simultaneously within a session, day, series of days or microcycle.
Aka concurrent / complex parallel.
How It Works
Fits early long-term athlete development (multilateral training), novice + developmental athletes.
Downside: total load must keep ↑ → may exceed tolerance.
Training Implication
Poor fit for intermediate–elite athletes → they need more variation + stimulus → emphasis/sequential models.
Topic 02 — Planning the Training Process · 2 of 3

Sequential Models

What It Is
Organize one or a limited number of training factors into a logical pattern toward a target outcome.
Built to fix parallel limits.
How It Works
Fixes: can't develop multiple abilities at once, long preparation needs, dense competition schedules.
Used in weightlifting, cycling, track + field, football, basketball, ice hockey, handball.
Training Implication
Best when athlete needs a greater stimulus than parallel gives. Rests on SFRA + residual training effects.
Topic 02 — Planning the Training Process · 3 of 3

Emphasis Models

What It Is
Parallel + sequential hybrid: multiple factors at once, varying emphasis over time.
Emphasis rotated about every 2 weeks.
How It Works
Some factors trained to adapt, some to maintain, some at minimal dose (some detraining).
Seen in track cycling, sprinting, MMA.
Training Implication
Ideal for intermediate–advanced athletes with congested schedules (also team + tactical athletes).

Topic 03 — Periodization Hierarchy

Multiyear & Macrocycle

Multiyear Plan
Most time, least detail.
E.g. college soccer player: freshman → senior goals.
Macrocycle
Months → up to a year; one per competitive season.
Football = 1 · college track = 2 (indoor/outdoor) · 3-sport HS athlete = 3.
Annual plans set the general pathway; macro → meso → micro cycles set the means, methods and modes.
Topic 03 — Periodization Hierarchy

Mesocycle & Microcycle

Mesocycle
2–6 weeks, usually 4.
Number per macrocycle depends on targets + macrocycle length.
Microcycle
Days → 2 weeks, usually 1 week (7 days).
Contains training days → sessions.

Four Periods

  • PreparatoryBase conditioning; no competition; ≈ off-season
  • 1st TransitionStone, O'Bryant & Garhammer addition — links prep → competition
  • CompetitivePeaking or maintenance
  • 2nd TransitionActive rest between seasons / macrocycles
Classic model: volume starts high and ↓, intensity starts low and ↑ — generalized curves; actual loading is nonlinear at micro/meso level.
Topic 03 — Periodization Hierarchy

Novice vs Advanced

Novice (Matveyev model)
Intensity ↑ gradually, volume ↓ gradually.
Not all novices tolerate large swings → smaller fluctuations.
Advanced
Train closer to ability, smaller adaptation windows.
Shift volume → intensity earlier in prep, higher overall volumes.
Zatsiorsky: a stimulating load for a novice is maintenance at best for an advanced athlete.

Preparatory

Topic 04 — Preparatory Period

The Preparatory Period

General Prep Phase
Early part. High volume, low intensity, big variety of means.
Builds general motor abilities + skills.
Specific Prep Phase
After general phase. Expands base via sport-specific activities.
Prepares athlete for the competitive period.
No competitions, limited technical/tactical work → ≈ off-season. Goal: base conditioning to tolerate more intense training.
Topic 04 — Preparatory Period · 1 of 2

Hypertrophy / Strength-Endurance

What It Is
Early prep (general phase). Low–moderate intensity, high volume.
50–75% 1RM · 3–6 sets × 8–20 reps
How It Works
Goals: ↑ work capacity + ↑ muscle mass → foundation for later phases.
Endurance athletes: ↑ strength-endurance without much hypertrophy.
Training Implication
Sprinter: 400 m runs, low-intensity plyos, back squat + leg curl. Daily variation + recovery weeks (usually phase end).
Topic 04 — Preparatory Period · 2 of 2

Basic Strength Phase

What It Is
Later prep (specific phase). Higher intensity, lower volume than hypertrophy phase.
80–95% 1RM · 2–6 sets × 2–6 reps
How It Works
Goal: ↑ strength of muscles essential to primary sport movements.
Exercises more sport-specific (squat, power clean, Bulgarian squat).
Training Implication
Sprinter: moderate-distance interval sprints + more complex plyos. Daily load variation facilitates recovery.

Topic 05 — First Transition & Competitive · 1 of 4

Strength / Power Phase

What It Is
Main phase of the first transition (≈ preseason). Links prep → competition.
30–95% 1RM (exercise-dependent) · 2–5 sets × 2–5 reps
How It Works
Shift: elevate strength + translate it into power. Mixed approach — heavy + low-load training.
Last week: ↓ volume and/or intensity for recovery.
Training Implication
Power-exercise loads don't follow %RM–rep rules: hang power clean ~80% 1RM · bench press throw ~50–70% 1RM.
Topic 05 — First Transition & Competitive

The Competitive Period

Goal
Prepare for competition: ↑ strength + power via ↑ intensity, ↓ volume.
Balancing act: cut too much → fatigue ↓ but fitness ↓.
Training Mix
Sport skills + tactics time ↑ sharply.
Physical conditioning (e.g. RT) time ↓ proportionally.
Two forms: peaking (1–2 weeks) or maintenance (team-sport seasons lasting months).
Topic 05 — First Transition & Competitive · 2 of 4

Peaking

What It Is
Peak condition for about 1–2 weeks. 50% to ≥93% 1RM · 1–3 sets × 1–3 reps
Very high → low intensity, very low volume.
How It Works
Taper: progressive shift from higher intensity → lower-intensity work to ↓ fatigue before competition.
Training Implication
Hold longer than ~2 weeks → ↓ fitness or overtraining → ↓ performance capacity.
Topic 05 — First Transition & Competitive · 3 of 4

Maintenance

What It Is
Team-sport season = many months. 85–93% 1RM · ~2–5 sets × 3–6 reps
Moderate–high intensity, low–moderate volume.
How It Works
Modulate load per microcycle by training, travel + competition schedule.
Monitor performance capacity + recovery.
Training Implication
Goal: maintain strength + power while managing fatigue from frequent competition.

Topic 06 — Second Transition

Active Rest

What It Is
Link between competition and next preparatory period / annual plan.
Aka active rest or restoration. Lasts 1–4 weeks.
How It Works
Avoid aggressive training right after peak/maintenance.
Rehab injuries; rest physically + mentally.
Sprinter example: volleyball, racket sports, leisurely swimming + very low-volume, non-specific RT at very low loads.
Topic 06 — Second Transition

Limits & Unloading

Limit
≤ 4 weeks unless injury recovery needs more.
Longer → much longer preparatory period to regain form.
Secondary Use
1-week unloading breaks between long phases (3 weeks) or periods.
Believed to ↓ overtraining potential.

Topic 07 — Sport Seasons & Periods

Off-Season & Preseason

Off-Season
End of postseason → ~6 weeks before first major competition (varies).
Mesocycle rotations of hypertrophy/SE + basic strength by sport + athlete needs.
Preseason
Leads into first major competition; strength/power focus.
Not the time to build foundations → that's the off-season.
Football player needs mass? → prescribe more hypertrophy mesocycles in the off-season.
Topic 07 — Sport Seasons & Periods

In-Season & Postseason

In-Season
All contests incl. tournaments. Long seasons (12–16+ weeks) → multiple mesocycles around key contests.
3–4 week blocks; unload in last microcycle before critical contests.
Postseason
Second transition: relative / active rest.
Longer postseason → more detraining → longer general prep next off-season.
Vary training days, intensity + volume across microcycles to keep preparedness high in congested schedules; monitor to fine-tune.

Topic 08 — Undulating vs Linear

The "Linear" Misnomer

Matveyev
Nonlinear, rhythmic load variation (Yakovlev's supercompensation).
A core tenet of periodization = remove linearity.
Traditional Model
Falsely called "linear" for gradual mesocycle intensity ↑.
Actually nonlinear in intensity + volume-load at micro level and across the mesocycle.
Same sets/reps + changing load → volume-load still undulates → not linear.
Topic 08 — Undulating vs Linear

Weekly vs Daily Undulating

WUP · 4-Week Mesocycle
Resistance training.
Week 1: 5 × 8 @ 70% 1RM
Week 2: 4 × 4 @ 85% 1RM
Week 3: 3 × 1 @ 95% 1RM
Week 4: Unloading / deload week
DUP · 1 Week of a 4-Week Mesocycle
Resistance training.
Monday: 5 × 8 @ 70% 1RM
Wednesday: 4 × 4 @ 85% 1RM
Friday: 3 × 1 @ 95% 1RM
Topic 08 — Undulating vs Linear

Which Is Better?

Pro-Undulating
No accumulated neural fatigue from long, ever-rising intensity.
Some studies favor it.
Pro-Traditional
Undulating → high volume-load → ↑ peripheral/metabolic fatigue, ↑ injury risk.
GAS/SFRA/fitness-fatigue → ↓ preparedness, possible overtraining in advanced athletes.
Research is mixed: some favor undulating, some show no difference, some favor traditional.

Core Idea
High concentration of training load on a limited number of motor + technical abilities.
Uses specialized mesocycle blocks — 3 special types.
Accumulation
Basic abilities (aerobic endurance, muscle strength).
High volume, low intensity.
3–4 weeks.
Transmutation
Sport-specific abilities.
High-intensity load.
3–4 weeks.
Realization
Maximal abilities.
Full restoration + taper for peak.
4 weeks.

Plan Design Rules

Mesocycle Logic
Block of 2–3 microcycles.
Unloading meso before next meso in off-season/preseason. Key microcycles in-season.
Vary volume-load, intensity, targeted exercises.
Loading Rules
RM loads NOT trained to failure → ↓ power, ↑ overtraining risk.
Use % RM ranges instead.
Targets are working sets → 3–5 warm-up sets first.
