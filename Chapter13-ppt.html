Topic 01 — Reasons for Testing

Why We Test

  • Baseline Establish baseline & identify strengths/weaknesses → guides needs analysis
  • Evaluate Evaluate program effectiveness → objective pre/post comparison across a training block
  • Motivate Motivate athletes → measurable goals, buy-in, tangible proof of progress
  • Predict Predict performance potential → talent identification, projection
  • Educate Educate athletes → data-driven feedback on where training focus is needed
  • Readiness Determine readiness → return-to-play clearance, safe load progression
Topic 01 — Reasons for Testing

Turning Data Into Decisions

Guides Program Design
Test results → needs analysis → exercise selection and priorities.
Weakest quality on the profile becomes the next block's emphasis.
Tracks Progress
Baseline + retest → objective measure of adaptation.
Feeds directly into periodization adjustments.
Testing without a clear purpose wastes athlete time — every test should answer one specific question.

Testing exists to answer specific questions about an athlete — never test without knowing what decision the data will drive.

Topic 02 — Testing Terminology

Core Terms

Field Test
Performed outside the lab, minimal equipment.
E.g. vertical jump, sprint, sit-and-reach.
Laboratory Test
Performed with specialized equipment under controlled conditions.
E.g. VO2max via metabolic cart, DEXA body composition.
Norm-Referenced Test
Compares an athlete's score to a reference population/percentile.
Criterion-Referenced Test
Compares an athlete's score to a fixed standard or cutoff.
Topic 02 — Testing Terminology

More Key Terms

Test Battery
Group of tests administered together to build a full athlete profile.
Pilot Testing
Trial run of a new protocol on a small sample before full rollout.
Exposes logistical issues before they cost real testing time.
Field tests trade lab precision for practicality — choose the tool that answers the question with the resources on hand.

Reliability and validity aren't optional vocabulary — they're the two criteria every test must satisfy before its numbers can be trusted.

Topic 03 — Validity

What Is Validity?

Validity = the degree to which a test measures what it purports to measure.

1
Face Validity
Does it look right?
2
Content Validity
Does it cover the full attribute?
3
Criterion Validity
Does it match a gold-standard measure?
4
Construct Validity
Does it reflect the underlying trait?
Topic 03 — Validity · 1 of 4

Face Validity — The Surface Check

What It Is
Subjective judgment that a test appears to measure the intended trait "on its face."
Weakest form of validity — no statistical backing.
How It Works
Coach, athlete, or reviewer visually judges relevance based on how closely the test resembles the skill.
Training Implication
Use it to build athlete buy-in — a test that visibly resembles the sport skill is easier to sell as meaningful. Never rely on face validity alone.
e.g. A vertical jump test “looks” like it measures leg power.
Topic 03 — Validity · 2 of 4

Content Validity — Covering the Whole Domain

What It Is
Extent to which a test covers all relevant facets of the attribute being measured.
How It Works
Established through expert review — content experts confirm the test items represent the full domain.
Training Implication
A single sprint test has poor content validity for "speed" if it ignores acceleration, top-end, and change of direction separately.
e.g. A speed battery testing acceleration, top speed, AND change of direction.
Topic 03 — Validity · 3 of 4

Criterion Validity — Matching the Gold Standard

What It Is
Degree to which scores correlate with a gold-standard criterion measure.
Concurrent (measured at the same time) or predictive (forecasts a future outcome).
How It Works
Correlation coefficient (r) calculated between the new test and the criterion; r ≥ .80 is generally acceptable.
Training Implication
Vertical jump (concurrent validity) as a field-test proxy for lab-measured leg power lets coaches skip the force plate without losing accuracy.
e.g. Vertical jump score correlated with force-plate peak power (r ≥ .80).
Topic 03 — Validity · 4 of 4

Construct Validity — Reflecting the Underlying Trait

What It Is
Degree to which a test reflects an underlying theoretical construct that can't be directly observed (e.g. "explosive power").
How It Works
Built over time via accumulated evidence — convergent (correlates with related measures) + divergent (doesn't correlate with unrelated ones).
Training Implication
Used when no true gold-standard exists — most performance constructs (agility, work capacity) rely on construct validity.
e.g. Jump + sprint + throw scores combine to confirm “explosiveness.”

Topic 04 — Reliability

What Is Reliability?

Reliability = the consistency/reproducibility of a measurement across repeated administrations.

1
Test-Retest Reliability
Same athlete, repeated over time
2
Intrarater & Interrater Reliability
Same tester vs. different testers
Topic 04 — Reliability · 1 of 2

Test-Retest — Consistency Over Time

What It Is
Consistency of scores when the same athlete performs the same test on separate occasions under the same conditions.
How It Works
Quantified via correlation/ICC between trial 1 and trial 2 scores. Biological variation, learning effect, and fatigue all threaten it.
Training Implication
Familiarization trials before the "real" testing day → reduce learning-effect noise → tighter test-retest reliability.
Topic 04 — Reliability · 2 of 2

Intrarater vs. Interrater

Intrarater Reliability
Consistency of scores when the same tester repeats measurement or scoring of the same data.
Tester re-measures recorded video/data blind to the first score; compared via ICC.
Interrater Reliability
Consistency of scores when different testers measure or score the same athlete/data.
Two or more testers independently score the same trial; compared via ICC — the most vulnerable form, since testers vary in technique and judgment.
Training Implication
Use one tester per session wherever possible — it sidesteps interrater disagreement entirely.
If multiple testers are required, standardize the protocol and train them together beforehand; a shared scoring rubric raises both intrarater and interrater reliability at once.
Topic 04 — Reliability

Quantifying Reliability

ICC — Intraclass Correlation Coefficient
Ranges 0–1.
≥ 0.90 = excellent. 0.75–0.89 = good. < 0.75 = questionable/poor.
SEM — Standard Error of Measurement
Expresses reliability in the test's own units. Smaller SEM = more precise.
Builds a confidence interval around a single score → separates real change from measurement noise.
A test can be perfectly reliable and still invalid — reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

Topic 05 — Test Selection Factors · 1 of 4

Sport — Reflecting Competitive Demands

What It Is
The test should reflect the demands and skills specific to the athlete's competitive sport.
How It Works
Analyze the sport's positional, movement, and energy demands before choosing a protocol (needs analysis).
Training Implication
A 40-yd dash suits a football combine; a 2000m row time trial suits rowing — mismatched tests produce data irrelevant to the sport.
Topic 05 — Test Selection Factors · 2 of 4

Movement — Matching Pattern & Plane

What It Is
The test's movement pattern should mimic or closely relate to the sport-specific movement.
How It Works
Match plane of motion, joint action, and skill complexity to the competition movement.
Training Implication
Testing linear sprint speed for a change-of-direction-dominant sport (e.g. tennis) underestimates true movement-specific capacity.
Topic 05 — Test Selection Factors · 3 of 4

Energy System — Stressing the Right Pathway

What It Is
Test duration and intensity should stress the same energy system(s) taxed during competition.
How It Works
Match the test to the phosphagen / glycolytic / oxidative demand profile of the sport.
Training Implication
Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery suits soccer's repeated-sprint/aerobic-base demand; a Wingate suits a track sprinter's phosphagen demand.
Topic 05 — Test Selection Factors · 4 of 4

Athlete Profile — Fitting the Individual

What It Is
Test selection accounts for the athlete's training age, injury history, position, and goals.
How It Works
Screen for contraindications; adjust test complexity to technical competency; use position-specific norms.
Training Implication
A novice shouldn't be tested with a 1RM back squat on day one — use a submaximal predicted-1RM protocol until competency is established.

Topic 06 — Environmental Factors

Control the Conditions

  • Climate Temperature & humidity → alters cardiovascular strain, fatigue onset
  • Altitude Altitude → ↓ VO2max, ↑ ventilation at the same absolute workload
  • Surface Testing surface → track vs. turf vs. court changes sprint/agility times
  • Timing Time of day → performance varies with circadian rhythm; retest at the same time
  • Equipment Equipment calibration → uncalibrated gates/force plates invalidate comparisons
  • Space Testing space → adequate room, lighting, and ventilation for safe max-effort testing

Standardize every environmental variable you can control — the goal is for the score to change, not the conditions.

Topic 07 — Health & Safety Considerations

Before Any Max Effort

  • Screen Pre-participation health screening (PAR-Q+ or equivalent) → flags contraindications
  • Clearance Medical clearance → required for flagged risk factors before max-effort testing
  • Consent Informed consent → athlete understands purpose, risks, and right to stop
  • EAP Emergency action plan → posted, rehearsed, and equipment (AED) accessible
  • Supervision Qualified supervision → certified staff present for all maximal tests
  • Contraindications Illness, recent injury, or uncontrolled blood pressure → delay testing

No test result is worth an athlete's safety — screening and an emergency action plan come before the stopwatch.

Topic 08 — Test Administration & Sequencing

Administration Principles

Standardize Everything
Same instructions, warm-up, equipment, and tester every session.
Removes procedural variance from the score.
Familiarize First
Practice trial before the scored trial.
Cuts the learning effect that inflates apparent "improvement."
Topic 08 — Test Administration & Sequencing

Recommended Test Sequence

1
Non-fatiguing tests
Resting HR/BP, height/weight, flexibility
2
Agility tests
T-test, pro agility (5-10-5)
3
Maximum power/strength tests
Vertical jump, 1RM
4
Sprint tests
10m/40-yd sprint, flying 20m
5
Local muscular endurance tests
Push-up test, YMCA bench press test
6
Anaerobic capacity tests
300-yd shuttle, Wingate test
7
Aerobic capacity tests
1.5-mile run, VO2max test
Fatiguing tests always come last — a tired athlete can't produce a valid max-effort score on anything after.
Topic 08 — Test Administration & Sequencing

Key Takeaway

Sequence is a validity issue, not a scheduling one — order tests from least to most fatiguing.

Topic 09

Preparing Athletes
for Testing

Topic 09 — Preparing Athletes for Testing

Pre-Test Guidelines

  • Recovery Avoid strenuous exercise 24–48 h prior → full recovery before max-effort testing
  • Routine Replicate normal sleep & diet → eliminates confound from fatigue or underfueling
  • Abstain Abstain from caffeine/alcohol per protocol → removes stimulant/depressant effect
  • Hydrate Arrive hydrated → dehydration impairs power & endurance performance
  • Gear Standardized footwear/clothing → same gear every session
  • Understand Understand test purpose & protocol → ↓ anxiety, ↑ technical execution
Topic 09 — Preparing Athletes for Testing

Familiarization & Consent

Familiarization Session
Practice trial(s) on unfamiliar tests before data collection.
Improves technique and the reliability of the real score.
Informed Consent
Athlete/guardian signs before participation.
Confirms understanding of purpose, procedures, and right to withdraw.

A well-prepared athlete produces a valid score — an unprepared one just produces noise.
