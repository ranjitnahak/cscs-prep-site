Topic 01 — The Warm-Up

The Case for Warming Up

  • Force ↑ Rate of force development, reaction time, and force output at higher velocities
  • Oxygen Bohr effect → higher temp releases more O₂ from hemoglobin/myoglobin
  • Blood Flow ↑ Blood flow to active muscles via vasodilation
  • Metabolism Faster ATP turnover, especially in anaerobic pathways
  • Mindset ↑ Psychological preparedness for performance
80%+ of published research shows warm-up improves performance.
Topic 01 — The Warm-Up

Two Families of Effects

Temperature-Related
↑ Muscle & core temperature.
Enhanced neural function.
Disruption of transient connective tissue bonds.
Non-Temperature-Related
↑ Blood flow to muscles.
↑ Baseline oxygen consumption.
Postactivation potentiation.
Active warm-ups (movement-based) beat passive (external heating) — they drive both effect families plus activity-specific metabolic pathways.
Topic 01 — The Warm-Up

No Single Perfect Warm-Up

Injury Risk
Evidence is unclear, but likely positive.
↑ Muscle temp → ↑ resistance to muscle tears.
Specificity Required
Structure & content must reflect activity, athlete, and logistics.
Many effective warm-ups exist — there's no single prescription.
Plan the warm-up around the activity, required physiological responses, the athlete, and logistics.

Topic 02 — Components of a Warm-Up

Four Progressive Processes

An effective warm-up builds progressively through four processes.

1
Stimulate
Cardiorespiratory & metabolic processes
2
Raise Temperature
Body & working-muscle temperature
3
Increase ROM
Relative to the upcoming activity
4
Increase Intensity
Muscle contraction intensity, progressively
Topic 02 — Components of a Warm-Up

The Traditional Two-Phase Structure

General Warm-Up
~5 min low-intensity aerobic activity (jog, skip, cycle).
↑ Heart rate, blood flow, deep muscle temp.
↓ Viscosity of joint fluids.
Specific Warm-Up
Movements matching the sport's biomechanical patterns.
Includes rehearsing the skills to be performed.
Static stretching used to follow the general phase — dynamic stretching has mostly replaced it.

The Raise, Activate and Mobilize, and Potentiate (RAMP) protocol

Topic 03 — RAMP Protocol

Competition vs. Training Warm-Up

Competition Warm-Up
Focused entirely on maximizing short-term performance.
Training Warm-Up
Optimizes acute performance and builds short-, medium-, and long-term development.
Athletes spend a lot of training time warming up. Planned well, that time builds real development — not just readiness.
Topic 03 — RAMP Protocol

The Three Phases of RAMP

Raise
Elevate physiological parameters.
Simultaneously build sport-skill patterns.
Activate & Mobilize
Develop movement competency.
Squat, lunge, and other key patterns.
Potentiate
Progressive intensity up to competition/session level.
RAMP builds on general/specific structure but explicitly targets short-, medium-, and long-term performance.
Topic 03 — RAMP Protocol · Raise

Raise — More Than Just Aerobic Activity

Looks Like General Warm-Up
↑ Body temp, heart rate, respiration, blood flow.
↓ Joint fluid viscosity.
But the Focus Differs
Not generic aerobic drills — activities that simulate upcoming movement patterns.
Builds skill capacity from the very first minute.
Topic 03 — RAMP Protocol · Activate & Mobilize

Mobility, Not Static Flexibility

Flexibility = static range of motion. Mobility = moving freely and effectively — combining motor control, stability, and flexibility.
  • Goal Develop athlete's movement competency, not lengthen muscles
  • Method Build the phase around key patterns (squat, lunge, etc.)
  • Timescale Longer-term consideration, not just next-session readiness
Topic 03 — RAMP Protocol · Activate & Mobilize

Static Stretching in Warm-Up — The Debate

The Concern
Can reduce force, power, running speed, and strength endurance.
Linked to ↓ musculotendinous stiffness / ↓ neural drive.
The Nuance
Short-duration static stretching (< 60 s) shows no detrimental effect.
Follow it with intense activity to offset any decrement.
Sports needing greater ROM (gymnastics, martial arts) may still benefit — weigh it with a benefit-risk analysis.
Topic 03 — RAMP Protocol · Potentiate

Potentiate — The Missing Phase

Progressive intensity → Treppe (staircase) effect → Motor neuron more sensitive to input → Muscle activated with less neural drive
Most critical for sports needing high speed, strength, or power — yet often omitted from traditional warm-ups.
  • Use Sport- or capacity-specific activities (e.g. speed & agility)
  • Progression Staged increase in intensity up to competition/session demand

Topic 04 — CNS Preparatory Exercises

What Is PAPE?

Postactivation performance enhancement (PAPE): a low-volume acute stimulus that briefly boosts neuromuscular performance afterward.
  • Premise Prior high-intensity activity can enhance motor unit recruitment & rate coding
  • Modalities Resistance training, resisted/assisted sprinting, isometrics, plyometrics
  • Outcome Most research shows effects on subsequent sprint and/or jump performance
Topic 04 — CNS Preparatory Exercises

Potentiation vs. Fatigue

Activities that generate the most potentiation also tend to generate the most fatigue — the fitness-fatigue balance.
Highly Individual
Response depends on stimulus type, intensity, volume, and timing.
Logistically Hard
Equipment and time constraints often rule out effective team-wide use.
Topic 04 — CNS Preparatory Exercises

Sample Preconditioning Combinations

Lower Body
Back squat (60–90% 1RM) → countermovement jump.
Resisted sprint (10–60% BW) → sprint.
Depth jump → countermovement jump.
Upper Body
Bench press (60–90% 1RM) → medicine ball throw.
Push press (40–80% 1RM) → shot put.
Rest before the potentiated activity: 1–12 minutes, adjusted to the athlete and session goals.
Topic 04 — CNS Preparatory Exercises

Guidelines & Precautions

  • Training Level Well-trained, high speed/power athletes respond best
  • Trade-Off Resistance protocols → greatest gain but also greatest fatigue
  • Accessibility Plyometrics & resisted sprints — lower potential, more usable in warm-ups
  • Transfer The closer the preconditioning activity is to the main activity, the greater the transfer
  • Decision Base use on a comprehensive cost-benefit analysis

Topic 05 — Flexibility

Static vs. Dynamic Flexibility

Static Flexibility
ROM about a joint during a passive movement.
No voluntary muscular activity — external force does the work.
Dynamic Flexibility
Available ROM during active movement.
Requires voluntary muscular action; generally > static ROM.
A normal ROM cannot guarantee normal movement — static flexibility does not directly transfer to sport performance.
Topic 05 — Flexibility

Optimal, Not Maximal

The most flexible athlete is not always the most successful — the goal is optimal, sport-specific flexibility.
  • Mobility Integrates balance, postural control, and perception through ROM
  • Both Extremes Risky Inflexibility AND hyperflexibility can increase injury risk
  • Force Over ROM Greater ROM can extend the time force is applied → ↑ impulse
Topic 05 — Flexibility

Factors Affecting Flexibility

Fixed by Biology
Joint structure (ball-and-socket > ellipsoidal > hinge).
Age & sex — younger and female athletes tend more flexible.
Tissue & Neural
Muscle, MTU, tendons, ligaments, fascia, skin.
Stretch tolerance & neural control (afferent/efferent).
Trainable
Resistance training through full ROM.
Activity level — stretching & full-ROM training maintain flexibility.
Topic 05 — Flexibility

Elasticity, Plasticity & Muscle Bulk

Elasticity
Returns to original resting length after a passive stretch.
Plasticity
Assumes a new, greater length after a passive stretch — the target of stretching.
A significant ↑ in muscle bulk can impede joint ROM (e.g. large biceps/deltoids limiting triceps stretch) — a trade-off for power athletes.
Topic 05 — Flexibility

The Stretch Reflex

Rapid stretch → Muscle spindle fires → Sensory neuron → Motor neuron in spine → Reflexive contraction of stretched muscle
Static stretching moves too slowly to trigger the reflex → muscle relaxes → greater stretch. Ballistic/dynamic stretching moves fast enough to trigger it → limits ROM.

Topic 06 — Types of Stretching

Three Broad Categories

Stretching techniques fall into three broad categories.

1
Static
Slow movement, held end position
2
Dynamic
Active, controlled movement through ROM
3
Precontraction (PNF)
Muscle actions before a passive stretch
Topic 06 — Types of Stretching · Static

Static Stretching

Active
Athlete's own muscles supply the force to reach end position.
Passive
A partner or machine supplies the external force.
Hold end position 10–30 seconds. Too slow to trigger the stretch reflex → lower injury risk than ballistic stretching, easy to learn, effective at ↑ ROM.
Topic 06 — Types of Stretching · Dynamic

Dynamic Stretching

  • Definition Controlled, moderate-paced active motion through a large ROM
  • Specificity Mimics the sport movement — e.g. walking knee lift mimics a sprinter's knee drive
  • Muscle State Muscle stays active through the ROM (unlike static, which relaxes)
  • Trade-Off Ideal for warm-up, but may be less effective than static/PNF for building static ROM
Topic 06 — Types of Stretching · Dynamic

Ballistic Stretching

A special type of dynamic stretch — uses intense bouncing movements to reach end ROM, rather than controlled motion.
Dynamic
Controlled, avoids bouncing.
Smaller ROM, but full active control throughout.
Ballistic
Triggers the stretch reflex → can limit ROM.
Higher injury risk, especially with prior injury history.
Topic 06 — Types of Stretching · PNF

Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation

Autogenic Inhibition
Active contraction of a muscle stimulates its own GTO.
→ Reflexive relaxation of that same muscle during the next stretch.
Reciprocal Inhibition
Contracting the opposing (agonist) muscle stimulates its GTO.
→ Simultaneous reflexive relaxation of the stretched (antagonist) muscle.
Topic 06 — Types of Stretching · PNF

Three PNF Techniques

Hold-Relax
Prestretch (10s) → isometric hold (6s) → passive stretch (30s).
Gain via autogenic inhibition.
Contract-Relax
Prestretch (10s) → concentric action through full ROM → passive stretch (30s).
Gain via autogenic inhibition.
Hold-Relax + Agonist Contraction
Same first two phases as hold-relax.
Athlete then concentrically contracts the agonist into the new ROM.

Topic 06 — Types of Stretching · PNF

The Most Effective Technique

Hold-relax with agonist contraction produces the greatest ROM gain — it stacks reciprocal inhibition (primary) and autogenic inhibition (secondary).

Topic 06 — Types of Stretching · PNF

Guidelines & Precautions

  • Contraction Moderate intensity, sustained 6–10 s
  • Stretch Passive stretch duration ~30 s
  • Volume 2–4 repetitions, 30 s rest between reps
  • Prep Ensure a thorough general warm-up beforehand
  • Communication Clear communication with partner is essential — especially for smaller joints
Topic 06 — Types of Stretching

Programming Considerations

15–30 s
Recommended static stretch duration; diminishing returns beyond ~30 s.
2–8 weeks
Short-term intervention window for measurable flexibility gains.
1x/week
PNF may be the exception — one session per week may be optimal.
Frequency (sessions/week) seems to matter more than single-session duration — total daily stretch time may matter as much as any one stretch.
Topic 06 — Types of Stretching

When to Stretch

Post-Practice
Within 5–10 min after practice, while muscles are warm.
↑ Collagen elasticity from residual heat; effect on soreness is mixed.
Separate Session
Precede with a thorough general warm-up.
Useful as a recovery-day session after competition.
