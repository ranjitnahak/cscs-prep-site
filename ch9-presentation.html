Detrimental
Overexcited/underexcited, or losing control of anger/frustration → ceases to function effectively
Key coaching goal: train athletes to tap into emotions to generate energy while maintaining a sense of control over them (64).
Topic 02 — Energy Management

Arousal — Intensity of Motivation

A blend of physiological + psychological activation (64). Always present, on a continuum: deep sleep / comatose ←→ highly excited. Not automatically pleasant or unpleasant — simply a measurement of activation.

  • HR Heart rate
  • BP Blood pressure
  • EEG Electroencephalography
  • EMG Electromyography
  • Self-report Activation–deactivation checklist (63)
Topic 02 — Energy Management

Anxiety — Trait vs State

TRAIT
Trait Anxiety
Acquired disposition to perceive nondangerous situations as threatening (58)
→
STATES
Arousal
General activation, deep sleep → intense excitement
↓
State Anxiety
Moment-to-moment nervousness, worry, apprehension (57)
↓
Cognitive
Worries, negative thoughts
Somatic

Trait anxiety primes the athlete to experience state anxiety (58) — high-trait-anxious athletes flood attentional capacity with task-irrelevant cognitions (failure, catastrophe, ego).
Topic 02 — Energy Management

Stress: Distress vs Eustress

Stress = substantial imbalance between demand (physical/psychological) and perceived response capability, where failure has real consequences (51). A stressor is the environmental or cognitive event that precipitates it.

Distress
Negative state → generates cognitive + somatic anxiety
Eustress
Positive state → generates positive mental energy + physiological arousal
Both generate arousal — but only distress (the negative perception) also generates anxiety.
Topic 02 — Recap

Managing the Energy Budget

✅ Emotions generate or drain mental energy — control matters more than avoidance
✅ Arousal is neutral activation; anxiety is arousal perceived negatively
✅ Trait anxiety primes state anxiety, which splits into cognitive + somatic

Distress and eustress both raise arousal — only distress adds anxiety. Managing perception is managing performance.

Topic 03 — Theory 1 of 5

Drive Theory (Hull)

The simplest model: as arousal/state anxiety ↑, so does performance (56) — a direct, linear relationship. Holds at low-to-moderate arousal, but breaks down when athletes get too pumped up.

Skill Level
↑ skill = wider latitude of optimal arousal. Novices are in the cognitive/analysis stage — attention already filled with movement details, so worry competes for the same capacity.
Task Complexity
Simple/well-learned skills tolerate high arousal (few task-relevant cues). Complex decision-making tasks (e.g. a goalie facing a shot) need optimized — not maximized — arousal.
Coaching cue: lower arousal + decision demands for developing athletes → simple, clear, direct instructions.
Topic 03 — Theory 2 of 5

Inverted-U Theory (Yerkes & Dodson)

Arousal facilitates performance up to an optimal point, beyond which further arousal reduces it (76). Most coaches intuitively accept this — flat and over-amped both feel like poor performance.

Skill / Experience
High skill/experience → higher, wider peak. Low skill/experience → narrower, earlier peak.
Personality
Introverted athletes peak at lower arousal. Extroverted athletes peak at higher arousal.
Task Complexity
Complex sport skill peaks at lower arousal. Simple sport skill peaks at higher arousal.
Criticism: the generic curve shape ignores exactly these individual influences of skill, ability, experience, and task complexity (20).
Topic 03 — Theory 3 of 5

Individual Zones of Optimal Functioning

Hanin (23): different people, in different performances, perform best at very different arousal levels.

Not a Midpoint
Ideal performance doesn't always sit at the center of the arousal continuum
Not a Single Point
Best performance occurs across a small range, or bandwidth, of arousal
Positive and negative emotions (excited/nervous) can both enhance performance; positive and negative emotions (comfortable/annoyed) can both debilitate it — the same emotion can be perceived differently by different athletes. In practice: athletes recall arousal tied to past performances of varying quality, then monitor and adjust before a big competition.
Topic 03 — Theory 4 of 5

Catastrophe Theory (Hardy)

Old assumption: increases beyond optimal arousal cause a gradual, proportionate decline. Reality: performance can suffer a severe, sudden drop — and calming down doesn't necessarily restore the prior level.

Somatic Arousal
Curvilinear, inverted-U relationship to performance
Cognitive Anxiety
Steady, negative relationship to performance
When physiological arousal rises in the presence of cognitive anxiety → sudden drop, not gradual decline. Practical implication: track cognitive anxiety, physiological arousal, and somatic anxiety as distinct, not interchangeable, constructs.
Topic 03 — Theory 5 of 5

Reversal Theory (Kerr)

How arousal and anxiety affect performance depends on the athlete's interpretation of that arousal — not the amount itself (30).

"

One athlete might interpret high arousal as excitement and readiness. Another, feeling the same emotion at the same level, might interpret it as unpleasant and reflective of a lack of confidence.

The power to reverse the interpretation — scary/worrisome → excitement/anticipation — sits within the athlete's control. This is why the way arousal affects performance is not fixed.
Topic 03 — Recap

Five Lenses on Arousal

✅ Drive: more arousal = better performance (until it isn't)
✅ Inverted-U: one optimal peak, shifted by skill/personality/task
✅ IZOF: optimal arousal is an individual bandwidth
✅ Catastrophe: decline can be sudden, not gradual
✅ Reversal: interpretation matters more than amount

No single curve fits every athlete. Coaches manage skill, task complexity, personality, and interpretation — not just arousal level.

Topic 04 — Motivation

Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Motivation

Motivation = the intensity and direction of effort (64). Athletes are rarely purely one or the other — they sit along a continuum depending on activity, perceived competence, and importance.

Intrinsic
Desire to be competent + self-determining (13). Driven by love of the game — exists regardless of material reward or punishment.
Extrinsic
Motivation from an external, individualized reward — awards, trophies, praise, social approval, fear of punishment.
To encourage intrinsic motivation: set goals that build perceived competence, and give athletes latitude in decision making to build perceived self-determination (13).
Topic 04 — Motivation

Achievement Motivation

McClelland (38): all athletes carry two opposing traits — the motive to achieve success and the motive to avoid failure.

MAS
Motive to achieve success. Pride in accomplishment; seeks challenge, ~50% probability of success.
MAF
Motive to avoid failure — really, avoid the perception of shame. Comfortable with very easy or near-impossible tasks.
Faced with a demanding goal (e.g. a hypertrophy phase lean-mass target): the MAF-dominated athlete may reduce effort and call the goal unrealistic; the MAS-dominated athlete heightens effort and perceives no threat.
Topic 04 — Motivation

Motivation via Self-Controlled Practice

Practice schedules that address autonomy, competence, and social relatedness can influence motivation itself (73).

"

Self-controlled practice involves the athlete in decisions related to practice structure — when to receive feedback, which skill to practice — or simply asking how they believe they're doing.

This promotes active involvement → enhances feelings of competence and autonomy (11, 12) → performance and learning improve. An easy, effective way to give athletes ownership of their own goals.
Topic 04 — Motivation

Reinforcement vs Punishment

An operant is the target behavior (e.g. correct footwork). Reinforcement increases its probability; punishment decreases a negative operant's probability.

Positive Reinforcement
Adds something positive (praise, prizes) to ↑ a good behavior
Negative Reinforcement
Removes something aversive (e.g. no wind sprints) to ↑ a good behavior
Positive Punishment
Adds something aversive (reprimand, sprints) to ↓ a bad behavior
Negative Punishment
Removes something valued (benching, privileges) to ↓ a bad behavior
Topic 04 — Motivation

Reward Over Punishment

How Should Reinforcement Be Applied?
Coaches should generally use a reinforcement strategy — help athletes focus on what they do correctly.
Punishment should be used sparingly — it increases the likelihood athletes focus on what they're doing wrong.
Under narrow attentional focus: positive reinforcement aids task-relevant cues, while punishment floods capacity with task-irrelevant cues.
Topic 04 — Recap

What Drives the Athlete

✅ Intrinsic motivation grows with perceived competence + autonomy
✅ MAS athletes seek challenge; MAF athletes avoid the shame of failure
✅ Self-controlled practice → ownership → better learning
✅ Reinforcement over punishment keeps focus task-relevant

Motivation is buildable — through autonomy, competence, and the right kind of feedback.

Topic 05 — Attention and Focus

Selective Attention

Attention = processing of external and internal cues that come to awareness. Selective attention suppresses task-irrelevant cues (crowd noise, planes overhead) to process what matters — commonly called an athlete's level of focus.

Topic 05 — Attention and Focus

Three Stages to Automaticity

Fitts and Posner (17): the ability to focus on task-relevant cues is a skill that improves with experience, across three stages.

1. Cognitive
Effortful, conscious regulation — the athlete has to think about the details of the task
2. Associative
Still focused on the task, but less concerned with the fine details of movement
3. Automaticity
Relaxed mind, skill executed without thinking — irrelevant cues filtered out automatically
Attaining automaticity — and the clarity of thought that comes with it — is a goal for every athlete, assuming proper instruction and coaching.
Topic 05 — Attention and Focus

Nideffer's Four Quadrants

Attentional style shifts along two dimensions: direction (internal ↔ external) and width (broad ↔ narrow) (41).

Assess
Broad · External
Reads the environment and its elements
Analyze
Broad · Internal
Processes information, develops strategy
Act
Narrow · External
Locks onto one or two external cues to execute
Prepare
Narrow · Internal
Mentally rehearses the upcoming action
Topic 05 — Attention and Focus

Coaching to the Right Quadrant

Understanding attentional styles improves coaching effectiveness — without it, athletes attend to inappropriate cues and react too slowly.

Overloaded by External Stimuli
Coach the athlete to narrow onto one important cue — e.g. an opponent's footwork
Lost in Their Own Head
Have the athlete practice describing out loud, to a coach, what they're feeling — e.g. during a lunge
Topic 05 — Recap

Focus Is a Trainable Skill

✅ Selective attention suppresses task-irrelevant stimuli — often called "level of focus"
✅ Cognitive → Associative → Automaticity: focus improves as skill is learned
✅ Assess / Analyze / Act / Prepare — four quadrants, matched to the moment

Working memory's limited capacity is an advantage in disguise — fill it with task-relevant cues and there's no room left for doubt.

Topic 06 — Relaxation Techniques

Diaphragmatic Breathing

"Belly breathing" — a basic stress-management technique and the precursor to virtually every other mental training method (63). Focuses attention on breath → clears the mind → increases concentration.

The Physiology
Deep inspiration + controlled expiration → shifts ANS toward parasympathetic/vagal tone → ↓ neural stimulation of muscles + organs → deep relaxation
The Technique
Relax the abdomen so it appears flaccid; each breath initiates with abdominal protrusion, through three stages: lower abdomen → mid-chest → upper chest
Can be combined with more dynamic relaxation techniques like PMR and autogenic training.
Topic 06 — Relaxation Techniques

PMR vs Autogenic Training

Progressive Muscle Relaxation
Tense each muscle maximally 10–15 s → consciously relax it completely → move to the next group. Practice in the days before competition, not the day of (28).
Autogenic Training
For injured or older athletes who find high tension uncomfortable: focus on the sensation of warmth and heaviness instead of a tense–relax cycle (64).
Both are designed to reduce physiological arousal and increase task-relevant focus — of extreme importance during complex or novel tasks, or high-pressure situations.
Topic 06 — Relaxation Techniques

Systematic Desensitization

Fears are learned by associating a previously neutral stimulus with a stressful event. SD (71) replaces the fear response with a relaxation response — a learned process called counterconditioning.

"

Build a hierarchy of feared scenes — for example, a gymnast's sequence from warming up before the event to the actual movement that caused a balance-beam injury — then visualize each scene while staying relaxed, progressing through the hierarchy in small, manageable doses.

Requires the athlete to already be reasonably skilled at both PMR and mental imagery.
Topic 06 — Relaxation Techniques

Reduce or Enhance?

How Should Athletes Use Arousal Control Techniques?
Use arousal reduction for a new skill, a complex one, or a high-pressure situation.
Use arousal enhancement for simple, well-learned skills, or situations of minimal pressure.
The goal: let the athlete perform with an unburdened mind, matching mental + physical intensity to the demands of the task.
Topic 06 — Imagery

Imagery

A cognitive skill in which the athlete creates or re-creates an experience in the mind — using visual, auditory, kinesthetic, olfactory, and even gustatory cues (64). Meta-analytic evidence supports its effectiveness for sport skill (16).

Simple → Complex
Start with a familiar static image (a golf ball, a tennis racket); vividness sharpens with practice
Internal vs External
First-person is specific to execution; third-person feels natural from reviewing film — either can work
Rehearsing successful execution under imagined competitive conditions → ↑ confidence + preparedness — especially valuable given how little real competitive time even starters accumulate relative to practice.
Topic 06 — Self-Efficacy

Self-Efficacy

Self-confidence = belief one can perform. Self-efficacy = that same belief, made situationally specific (4). Highly self-efficacious athletes don't doubt their ability, even after failure.

  • 1 Performance accomplishments — past success or failure
  • 2 Vicarious experiences — watching others (modeling)
  • 3 Verbal persuasion — encouragement from self or others
  • 4 Imaginal experience — using imagery to see oneself perform
  • 5 Physiological states — arousal perceived as facilitative or debilitative
  • 6 Emotional states — affect or mood
Topic 06 — Self-Talk

Self-Talk

Intrapersonal communication — the inner dialogue we have with ourselves (64). Spontaneous, or purposefully used to shift mood or behavior.

Positive
Encouraging, motivational, reinforcing — generally favorable emotions
Negative
Anger, discouragement, doubt — most consistently linked to poor performance
Instructional
Specific performance cues — can interfere with automaticity for expert performers
Topic 06 — Goal Setting

The Power of Goal Setting

Progressively challenging standards of performance, pursued with a defined criterion, that increase the likelihood of perceived success (35).

Directs Attention
Prioritizes where effort goes
Increases Effort
Success is contingent on goal attainment
Increases Reinforcement
Feedback ties directly to goal achievement
Topic 06 — Goal Setting

Process vs Outcome Goals

Process
Fully within the athlete's control — form, technique, daily actions. Success is strongly tied to effort.
Outcome
Little athlete control — winning, ranking, medals. Contingent on the efforts and abilities of others too.
Best practice blends both — a process-and-outcome orientation beats a winning-only attitude, which can narrow attentional focus and cause missed cues.
Topic 06 — Goal Setting

Short-Term vs Long-Term Goals

Short-Term
Close to current ability → frequent success → builds confidence, self-efficacy, motivation; counters boredom and frustration
Long-Term
Overarches the series of linked short-term goals; gives meaning and direction to the daily grind
Specificity matters: "25 minutes at 160–170 bpm" is a far more useful goal than "go out for a run" — vague goals give vague feedback.
Topic 06 — Managing Pressure

Yips, Choking, Flow & Clutch

Yips / Choking
Anxiety interferes with fluid movement patterns during a critical moment; the technical term is focal dystonia
Flow
Effortless movement, complete trust, remarkable self-control — the positive counterpart to choking
Clutch
Purposeful focus + optimal emotional control + high effort — often unpleasant to experience in the moment
Simone Biles described losing air awareness mid-rotation during the twisties — a dangerous break in spatial focus. Caitlin Clark described flow as staying fully present: "being where your feet are." Breathing, attentional cuing, and presence-centered focus all help toward flow and clutch — but yips and choking trace back to fight-or-flight, and sometimes trauma, so professional support is warranted.
Topic 06 — Recap

The Coach's Toolkit

✅ Relaxation techniques ↓ arousal + ↑ task-relevant focus
✅ Imagery rehearses success using every sense available
✅ Self-efficacy + self-talk shape the athlete's confidence loop
✅ Goals direct attention, effort, and reinforcement
✅ Flow and clutch are trainable through focus + emotional control

Mental skills are like physical skills — taught, practiced, and evaluated for effectiveness, not left to chance.

Topic 07 — Mental Health

What Is Mental Health?

"

Mental health encompasses emotional, psychological, and social well-being (5, 47, 62). It is not simply the absence of mental illness.

Challenges are often first detected when sport, training, or life disrupts an athlete's normal self-care behavior — a holistic monitoring program addresses self-care, recovery, and psychological, behavioral, and emotional factors together (5).

Topic 08 — Psychological Impact of Injury

The Toll of Injury

Whether a minor setback or a career-ending injury, the psychological impact is significant (39).

Cognitive Responses
Recurrent intrusive thoughts, decreased self-esteem, lowered athletic identity (14)
Emotional Responses
Anger, fear, confusion, depression, helplessness, extreme guilt (1, 14)
Pushing through pain is often falsely viewed as heroic — and expected by teammates, parents, coaches, and fans (63).

✅ Personality + stress history + coping resources shape injury risk itself
✅ Personal + situational factors shape how the athlete appraises the injury
✅ Communication, tailoring, and self-efficacy speed a healthy return

Physical readiness and mental readiness are two different clocks — support both.

Topic 09 — Practice Fundamentals

Learning vs Performance

Learning
A relatively permanent change in the capability to perform a motor skill (50)
Performance
Execution of the skill in the current environment — affected by arousal, motivation, and more (29)
Topic 09 — Practice Schedule

Whole vs Part Practice

Whole Practice
Addresses the skill in its entirety — favored when subcomponents are highly interrelated (e.g. a lunge)
Part Practice
Breaks the skill into subcomponents — favored for challenging skills with low interrelatedness (e.g. the snatch: pull → transition → pull → catch)
Segmentation
Clear breaks between subcomponents
Fractionalization
Subcomponents practiced simultaneously but separately (arms vs legs)
Simplification
Adjusts difficulty — e.g. a PVC pipe for the snatch
Topic 09 — Practice Schedule

Three Ways to Reassemble the Skill

Pure-Part
Each subcomponent practiced multiple times independently, then the full skill
Progressive-Part
First two parts together, then add the third — building cumulatively
Repetitive-Part
First part alone, then each subsequent part is added on top
Topic 09 — Practice Schedule

Random & Variable Practice

Random Practice
Multiple skills practiced in random order in one session (vs blocked). Performance dips short-term — but learning improves (53).
Variable Practice
Variations of the same skill in one session (e.g. jumping to boxes of varying heights). Impairs session performance, but enhances performance on novel variations (33).
A blend — a few reps of each skill before switching — maximizes the benefits of both blocked and random practice (32).
Topic 09 — Practice Schedule

Observational Learning

Learning through watching a prerecorded video or live demonstration — of a novice or an expert. Combined with physical practice, it enhances learning (52); in S&C, partner work lets one athlete observe during the other's rest.

Internal vs External Coaching Cues
Fewest words, greatest impact. Overcoaching ("verbal overshadowing") disrupts fluid motion. External cues ("push through the floor") work best early in learning; internal cues ("breathe," "back straight") suit later refinement.
Topic 09 — Instructions

Three Instructional Styles

Explicit
Prescriptive "rules" for executing the task — full detail on body position and movement
Guided Discovery
The goal + key prompts to prevent injury — athlete explores the movement–goal relationship
Discovery
The goal only, little direction — can slow learning but frees up attention for task-relevant cues (75)
Topic 09 — Feedback

Intrinsic vs Augmented Feedback

Intrinsic
From the athlete's own senses — e.g. sensing a missed box on a squat box jump
Augmented
From an observer or technology — a coach's note, video, or force-plate data
Knowledge of Results
Info about the outcome — completion time, normative comparison to others
Knowledge of Performance
Info about the movement pattern itself — video, force-plate breakdown
Topic 09 — Feedback

Timing & Frequency

Concurrent feedback ↑ performance but impairs learning → best saved for competition.

Feedback given after task execution facilitates learning (69).

Frequent feedback helps early in skill learning, then should decrease as athletes progress — except for complex skills, which may need frequent feedback for longer (74).
