Training Principles First

Specificity
Training effects are limited to the systems used and overloaded in training.
Design to improve respiratory, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal & nervous systems.
Overload
A system must be stressed beyond what it is used to; adaptation stops once it is no longer overloaded.
Frequency, duration & intensity are the variables manipulated.
MED vs. MTD
Minimum effective dose (MED) — the least stimulus that maximizes adaptation while minimizing damage.
Maximum tolerated dose (MTD) — the most work that still gives measurable benefit; side effects (microtrauma, fatigue) are high.

VO2max — Maximal Aerobic Capacity

Why It Matters
The longer the event, the greater the share of energy that must come from aerobic metabolism.
High correlation between VO2max and endurance performance.
Its Limits
Other factors can be equally or more important: high LT, good exercise economy, efficient fat use, high % type I fibers.
In well-trained athletes, improving VO2max helps only up to a point.
Training Implication
Sustaining higher velocities can matter more than marginal VO2max gains — hence HIIT.
HIIT can improve peak power output, ventilatory threshold, H+ buffering, fat use and economy.

Lactate Threshold — The Fatigue Ceiling

What It Is
Speed or %VO2max at which a set blood lactate is seen (e.g., 4 mmol/L) or where lactate rises above resting levels.
Among athletes with similar VO2max, the best sustains the highest %VO2max without lactate build-up.
Better Than VO2max
LT appears to be a better indicator of endurance performance than VO2max.
Both LT and MLSS must be improved — this needs some training at elevated lactate.
Topic 01 — Factors Related to Aerobic Endurance Performance · 5 of 6

MLSS — Maximal Lactate Steady State

Definition
Intensity where maximal lactate production = maximal lactate clearance.
Marks the transition from the heavy to the severe domain.
Heavy vs. Severe
Heavy domain: lactate rises, then plateaus at a steady state.
Severe domain: lactate climbs continually until failure.
Key Point
MLSS = critical power (CP) = critical speed (CS).
Considered by many a better indicator of performance than VO2max or LT.
Worked Example — Critical Speed (illustrative)
Trial 1: 1,200 m in 4:00 (240 s) · Trial 2: 3,600 m in 14:00 (840 s)
CS = (3,600 − 1,200) ÷ (840 − 240) = 2,400 ÷ 600 = 4.0 m/s (14.4 km/h)
D′ = 1,200 − (4.0 × 240) = 240 m

Exercise Economy — Energy Cost at a Given Velocity

Running
Better performers have a slightly shorter stride length and greater stride frequency.
Cycling
Affected by body mass, velocity and aerodynamic position — more of each means more wind resistance and worse economy.
Swimming
Elite swimmers use less O2 at any velocity.
Biggest gains come from more efficient technique.
Key Point
Improved economy can enhance both VO2max and lactate threshold — training for it is critical.

Topic 02 — Exercise Mode & Training Frequency · 1 of 3

Step 1 — Exercise Mode

Match the Sport
Choose activities that mimic the competition movement pattern.
The more specific the mode, the greater the likely performance gain.
Why
Specific muscle-fiber recruitment and energy-system adaptations must be challenged.
Exceptions
Multi-sport athletes or general fitness: cross-training may be warranted.
Female athletes need not be trained differently — males and females respond similarly.
Topic 02 — Exercise Mode & Training Frequency · 2 of 3

Step 2 — Training Frequency

What Drives It
Interaction of intensity & duration, training status and sport phase.
Higher intensity/longer duration = fewer sessions; lesser-trained athletes need more recovery days.
Typical Numbers
Off-season: 3–4 days/week, progressing to daily (or multiple sessions/day) in preseason.
Must train more than twice per week to improve VO2max.
Too Much / Too Little
Too much = injury, illness, overtraining (above MTD). Too little = no adaptation (below MED).
Fewer sessions maintain fitness than were needed to build it. Multiple sessions/day: advanced or elite only.
Topic 02 — Exercise Mode & Training Frequency · 3 of 3

Recovery Between Sessions

Why It Matters
Adaptation happens during rest — heavy frequency can blunt it.
Priorities: rest, rehydration (fluids + electrolytes), fuel (carbohydrate), protein for repair.
Recovery Session
Active recovery: short, low intensity (zone 1) — not an extra training stimulus.
Must not be an extensive/LSD session just because it is easy.
Monitoring
Recovery sessions still count toward total training load and belong in every plan.
Athletes often know recovery methods but do not use them; elite athletes use more.

Topic 03 — Training Intensity · 1 of 8

Heart Rate — The Most-Used Method

Why It Works
Close link between HR and VO2 between 50–90% of VO2max (also called HRR).
Most accurate: lab-test the HR at the target %VO2max or LT.
Estimating HRmax
Fox equation: 220 − age; SD of 10–12 bpm.
HRR = HRmax − HRrest.
Caveats
Heat, humidity and altitude raise HR; mode and fitness also matter.
As fitness improves, a fixed HR becomes a lower relative stress — retest and adjust.
Topic 03 — Training Intensity · 2 of 8

Karvonen & %HRmax — Worked Examples

Karvonen Method
THR = (HRR × intensity) + RHR.
Age 30, RHR 60, 60–70%: HRmax 190 → HRR 130.
Low = (130×0.60)+60 = 138 · High = (130×0.70)+60 = 151 bpm
% Max HR Method
THR = APMHR × intensity.
Age 20, 70–85%: HRmax = 200.
Low = 200×0.70 = 140 · High = 200×0.85 = 170 bpm

RPE — Perceived Exertion

How It Works
Subjective rating on a 1–10 scale.
Valid for regulating intensity even when fitness changes.
Influences on RPE
Age, sex, training status, fitness; music/TV, temperature, altitude, nutrition, external feedback.
Still a valid monitoring tool.
Best Use
Especially helpful when HR is elevated by heat, humidity or altitude.

METs & Intensity Technology

METs
1 MET = 3.5 mL/kg/min O2 — the resting requirement; 10 METs = 10× resting.
Needs known/estimated VO2max; typically limited to clinical populations.
Power & Devices
Power meters (cranks, hubs, stride power) give reproducible intensity regardless of conditions.
Check validity, accuracy, reliability; assign zones that match training status.
Indoor vs. Outdoor
Treadmill running at slower speeds can feel easier with lower HR; outdoor performance is often superior.
Allow a familiarization period for new devices.
Topic 03 — Training Intensity · 7 of 8

Training Zones — Three-Zone Model

Zones
Typically 3–7 zones, referenced to lactate or ventilatory threshold.
Each zone = an intensity + a training goal.
Lactate Anchors
Low zone: stable lactate < 2 mmol/L.
Threshold zone: steady-state lactate 2–4 mmol/L.
High zone: > 4 mmol/L.
Zone Boundaries
Three-zone model based on ventilatory thresholds (VT1/VT2); LT and MLSS (critical power) mark the boundaries.

Topic 04 — Exercise Duration & Progression · 1 of 3

Step 4 — Exercise Duration

Inverse Link
The longer the session, the lower the intensity.
Above MLSS
E.g., ~85% VO2max: only 20–30 min before lactate-driven fatigue.
Well Below MLSS
E.g., ~70% VO2max: several hours before fatigue.
Topic 04 — Exercise Duration & Progression · 2 of 3

Step 5 — Exercise Progression

The 10% Rule
Increase frequency, intensity or duration by no more than ~10% per week.
Small steps add up to large gains over months and years.
Maintenance
Aerobic fitness holds for up to 5 weeks at as few as 2 sessions/week if intensity is maintained.
One Variable at a Time
Progress only one variable at a time.
If frequency/duration are capped by work or family, lean on intensity — and adjust zones as fitness changes.
Topic 04 — Exercise Duration & Progression · 3 of 3

When to Exceed 10%

Cyclist Example
68 mi/week over 3 sessions: +10% on the 18-mile tempo & interval rides (~2 mi each).
The 32-mile extensive ride (1 h 46) can safely rise 15–20 min (~6 mi, ≈ 20%).
Cautions
Only for a short time, with overtraining/injury monitoring and extra recovery.
Planned bumps = short-term functional overreaching.
Use Time, Not Distance
10 flat miles in 1:10, then 11 hilly miles in 1:58: distance +10%, time +65%.
Time accounts for terrain and conditions.

Topic 05 — Types of Aerobic Endurance Training Programs · 2 of 6

Extensive Endurance Training

What It Is
Formerly "long slow distance (LSD)" — but the goal is to extend aerobic capability, not just go slow.
Distance ≥ race distance or 30 min–2 h. Not practical beyond ~marathon distance.
Intensity
Zone 2 — "conversation" pace you could hold indefinitely.
Prioritize duration; do not raise speed and duration together.
Benefits
Cardiovascular & thermoregulatory function, mitochondrial/oxidative capacity, fat use (sparing glycogen), better LT; some IIx → I shift.
~80% of elite runners' yearly distance is at this pace.
Topic 05 — Types of Aerobic Endurance Training Programs · 3 of 6

Pace/Tempo Training

What It Is
Intensity at or slightly above race pace ≈ MLSS — "threshold training".
Steady: continuous ~20–30 min. Intermittent: tempo/cruise intervals, same intensity, brief rests.
Rules
Do not exceed the prescribed pace; if it feels easy, add distance, not speed.
Same muscle-fiber recruitment as competition.
Benefits
Improves running economy, LT and MLSS; builds a sense of race pace.
Topic 05 — Types of Aerobic Endurance Training Programs · 4 of 6

Interval Training

Prescription
Near-VO2max work bouts of 3–5 min (as short as 30 s).
Rest equal to work: W:R = 1:1.
Why
Allows more time near VO2max than continuous work.
Very stressful — use sparingly and only after a firm aerobic base.
Benefits
Increased VO2max and enhanced anaerobic metabolism.
Topic 05 — Types of Aerobic Endurance Training Programs · 5 of 6

HIIT — High-Intensity Intervals

Definition
Repeated bouts above MLSS / critical VO2 with brief recovery.
Tabata: 7–8 × 20 s work / 10 s rest to exhaustion.
Design
Spend several minutes above 90% VO2max per session.
Short (<45 s) = power & economy; long (2–4 min) = more glycolysis & lactate metabolism.
Long-interval example: ≥2–3 min at ≥90% with ≤2 min passive/easy recovery.
Evidence & Rest
Improves VO2max (beginners, trained), LT and economy; less clear in well-trained.
Rest too short = poor quality & injury risk; too long = loses glycolytic benefit. Takes less time than traditional training.
Topic 05 — Types of Aerobic Endurance Training Programs · 6 of 6

Fartlek — "Speed Play"

What It Is
Swedish for speed play — a blend of extensive, tempo and interval training.
Works for running, cycling and swimming.
Sample Run
Easy running (~70% VO2max) combined with hill work or short fast bursts (~85–90% VO2max).
Benefits
Challenges all systems and breaks monotony; enhances VO2max, LT, economy and fuel use.
A sound plan uses all types across the week, month and year.

Step 3 — VO2max → Maximal Aerobic Speed (MAS)

VO2max → MAS (km/h)
MAS (km/h) = VO2max ÷ 3.5
Our athlete: 43.1 ÷ 3.5 = 12.3 km/h.
km/h → m/s
m/s = km/h ÷ 3.6 (i.e., × 5/18).
Our athlete: 12.3 ÷ 3.6 = 3.4 m/s.
Why m/s
Interval distances and timings are set in metres and seconds — 3.4 m/s ≈ 68 m in 20 s.

Off-Season — Base Training

Priority
Build a large base of cardiorespiratory fitness: start long-duration, low-intensity; raise intensity (and less so duration) mid-to-late.
Progression
Intermediate/advanced: no more than 5–10% per week. Beginners starting very low may exceed 10%.
Beginner runner: 3 × 1 mile → +0.5 mile on 2 days = +33%.
Monitor
Cyclists (low impact) can often exceed 10%: 25 mi/wk +1.25–2.5 mi = only 7.5–15 min.
Track fatigue, soreness, resting HR and HRV; adding too much too fast can reduce performance.

Postseason — Active Rest

Purpose
Recover from the competitive season: low duration and intensity.
Still Do
Enough activity to maintain cardiorespiratory fitness, strength and lean mass.
Also
Rehabilitate in-season injuries and strengthen weak or underconditioned muscle groups.
A sound year-round plan is divided into seasons with specific goals that improve performance gradually and progressively.

Topic 08 — Cross-Training, Detraining, and Tapering · 1 of 4

Cross-Training

Uses
Maintain conditioning during injury or recovery from a cycle; reduce overuse risk by spreading stress to other muscle groups.
Multi-event athletes use it to maximize swim/bike/run.
Limits
To maintain VO2max, it must match primary-mode intensity and duration.
It will not improve single-event performance as much as mode-specific training.
Topic 08 — Cross-Training, Detraining, and Tapering · 2 of 4

Detraining

What Happens
Training duration/intensity falls or stops (break, injury, illness) and adaptations regress rapidly toward pre-training levels.
Minimizing Loss
Other modes only attenuate losses.
Best: keep the primary mode at reduced frequency and intensity.
Topic 08 — Cross-Training, Detraining, and Tapering · 3 of 4

Tapering

Definition
Systematic reduction of training duration & intensity with more emphasis on technique and nutrition to peak at competition.
Length & Evidence
Typically 7–28 days. Most research is in swimmers; runners and cyclists also benefit.
Benefits
Aids recovery and rehydration; boosts muscle and liver glycogen.
Volume falls while intensity and frequency are maintained.

Does Resistance Training Help?

The Worry
Fear of non-functional muscle mass and of the interference phenomenon keeps it overlooked.
The Evidence
Improves time-trial, middle- and long-distance performance in running, cycling, XC skiing and swimming — plus strength, power, running economy and sprint speed.
1,500–10,000 m running performance improved.
What Works
Heavy and explosive/plyometric training improve economy via stronger running muscles.
Max-strength > max-power > submax strength. Usually no overtraining or body-mass gain.

Exercise Selection, Intensity & Frequency

Exercises
Free-weight, multijoint lifts; plyometrics (hops, bounds, jumps, drop jumps, squat jumps).
See Ch. 18–19 for program design/technique.
Load & Frequency
Heavy resistance or plyometrics 2–3×/week.
Keep loads high (≥90% 1RM or ≤4RM) but weekly volume low to limit carry-over fatigue.
Maintenance
Preparatory-phase strength can be kept up to 12 weeks with 1 session/week.

Recovery & Training Sequence

Recovery
Shorter gaps = more fatigue carried over, higher cost, lower adaptation.
Some fatigue is needed; but maximize recovery, especially same-day. Monitor HR, RPE, power, lactate.

Findings are mixed. Train the highest priority first, while fresh; vary the order across phases of the year.

Altitude — Performance & Training

Acute Exposure
Performance decrements can begin as low as 700 m.
Acclimatization: 12–14 days up to 2,300 m, but may take months.

Needs a hypoxic dose of ≥12 h/day for ≥3 weeks at ~2,100–2,500 m.

Live at 2,000–3,000 m, train at or near sea level — get acclimatization benefits and sea-level neuromuscular quality.

Heat Acclimation — Why & What Adapts

The Problem
Un-acclimatized heat work raises thermoregulatory/cardiovascular strain, fatigue and perceived effort.
Adaptations
↑ plasma & blood volume, better skin blood flow, earlier sweat at lower core temp, higher sweat rate & cooling, less GI distress.
Result
More cardiovascular stability, lower core/skin temperature and reduced HR.
Topic 10 — Altitude and Heat Acclimation · 4 of 6

Heat Protocols

Duration of Protocol
Short <7 d · medium 8–14 d · long >15 d.
~75% of adaptation in the first 4–6 days; rest after 7+. Longer for multiday events and lesser-trained athletes.
Per Session
Aim for 60 min, max 90 (120 effective). Twice daily is no better than once.
Consecutive days work best; missing 1–3 days of 10–14 is unlikely to matter.
Conditions
Core/skin temperature and sweating must rise; use WBGT >30 °C (86 °F).
Use RPE — HR and pace mislead early on.

Passive Heat & Monitoring

Passive Exposure
Sauna or hot-water immersion after exercise: 30 min, ideally consecutive days.
Can reduce the volume of heat exercise needed.
Markers of Adaptation
Lower HR and core temperature for a given workload, higher sweat rate, better aerobic capacity.
Fitter athletes adapt faster and retain longer.

Heat Safety

Precautions
Start well hydrated and stay hydrated; have cooling ready (cold-water bath, ice vests); post signs/symptoms of exertional heat illness.
Planning
Reduce load initially, then build; monitor everyone, especially beginners.
Practice protocols months before and rehearse a practice event in expected conditions.

Topic 11 — Aerobic Endurance Training Exercises · 2 of 7

Stationary Bike

Set-Up
Seat height: knee slightly flexed (25–30°) at the bottom; knee over pedal center; hips do not rock.
Handlebars: elbows slightly flexed; arm-to-torso ~90°.
Pedaling
Balls of feet on pedals; neutral spine, shoulders not rounded.
Hands: pronated (upright), neutral (more lean), racing (max lean).
Errors
Toes only or rocking = seat too high; knees above hips at the top = too low; reaching for bars = seat too far back/bars too low.
Topic 11 — Aerobic Endurance Training Exercises · 3 of 7

Rowing Machine

Start & Drive
Back upright, slight hip lean; arms extended; shins near vertical.
Drive: knees → hips → arms.
Finish & Recovery
Knees extended, torso slightly back, handle at abdomen below the ribs.
Recovery is the reverse: arms → hips → knees.
Errors
Arms and legs together or arms-only pulling (legs give most power).
Handle over the knees / chain whipping = out-of-sync recovery.
Topic 11 — Aerobic Endurance Training Exercises · 4 of 7

Stair Stepper

Technique
Hold rails stepping on; whole foot on each pedal; upright posture with 4–8 in (10–20 cm) deep steps; hips level.
Do not let the steps hit the floor or upper stop; hold rails lightly.
Errors
Leaning on the machine (level too high).
Small steps on the toes at high frequency (steps too shallow).
Topic 11 — Aerobic Endurance Training Exercises · 5 of 7

Elliptical Trainer

Technique
Upright torso over hips; feet in full contact; knees not past toes.
Release the rails and pump arms like walking/running when balance allows.
Variations
Raise incline for running, lower for walking. Forward = more quads; backward = more hamstrings & glutes.
Errors
Heel lift / forefoot pressure; leaning on rails (disengages core) or hands too low.

Walking & Running Gait

Body Position
Head up, shoulders relaxed; ear–shoulder–hip aligned.
Running: 2–4° lean from heels to head.
Foot Strike & Stride
Walking: heel first, rolling heel-to-ball. Running: land at/under the hips (heel, mid or forefoot) to avoid braking.
Free hip movement; lift knees; engage hips/glutes.
Arm Action
Reciprocal with legs; no crossing the midline.
Fast walking: elbows 90°. Running: ±20° hand oscillation may improve economy.

Freestyle Swimming

Principle
Performance = swim-specific fitness + technique; poor technique raises drag and loses power.
Best route: a qualified swim coach.
Common Errors
Swinging hand entry crossing the centerline.
Early hand entry → short stroke.
