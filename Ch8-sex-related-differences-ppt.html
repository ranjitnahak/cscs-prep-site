Topic 01 — Implications of RT for Females

Why Resistance Training Matters

Health
↓ risk of degenerative disease (e.g. osteoporosis)
Injury Risk
↓ injury rates with regular participation
Performance
Enhances overall sporting performance
Evidence clearly shows females tolerate and adapt to resistance exercise stress — RT is now considered an essential component of any exercise or training program females follow.
Topic 01 — Implications of RT for Females

Physical Changes Continuing Into Adulthood

Skeletal
Growth plates close ≈ age 20 · peak bone density ≈ age 30 — neither undeveloped enough to raise injury risk from high-load training
Neural
Brain develops through 20s (mostly frontal lobe/decision-making) · motor areas already mature · visual-perceptual skills improve into early 30s
Training Link
Reactive agility training → improves visual-perceptual skills → ↓ fall risk later in life

Topic 01 — Implications of RT for Females

Maintaining Muscle & Bone Across the Lifespan

Without RT
Muscle mass + bone density decline begins mid-30s → accelerates ≈ age 50, especially with menopause
With RT
Long-term programs counteract skeletal/muscular aging changes
Beyond Performance
Extends years in sport · ↓ injury risk · maintains independence in activities of daily living into old age

A key focus of any RT program for a female should be to maintain muscle mass and bone mineral density — critical for both performance and lifelong health.

Topic 01 — Implications of RT for Females

Pregnancy: A Unique Training Context

Pregnancy occurs only after pubertal sexual development completes and before menopause (avg. age range 12–51). Significant physiological and anatomical changes occur across each trimester and postpartum — all of which can affect training and performance.
All major guidelines agree: exercise is a minimal-risk activity with benefits for most females during pregnancy — with modifications as pregnancy progresses.

Trimester-by-Trimester Changes

Third Trimester & Postpartum

Third
Cardiorespiratory + Musculoskeletal
Blood volume ↑ 50% by 34 weeks · peripheral vascular resistance ↑ from 32 weeks · ↑ lumbar lordosis + foot size · compression neuropathies
10 Wks Postpartum
Recovery
Most cardio/respiratory changes return to baseline within 1–2 weeks · pelvic floor dysfunction + urinary incontinence + ligament laxity may persist up to 5 months
Topic 01 — Pregnancy · Program Design

Exercise Recommendations by Stage

1st–2nd Tri
Continue preprepgnancy activity/intensity with monitoring · initiate + continue pelvic floor exercises · avoid supine positions from 2nd trimester on
3rd Tri
Modify based on signs/symptoms + comfort · continue avoiding supine positions · modify for neuropathy discomfort
Postpartum
Pelvic floor + core stabilization immediately, 12–24 wks · gradual return to sport · Cesarean deliveries may need extra time for abdominal strength
Program design (exercise selection, frequency, intensity, duration) should be based on the athlete's baseline fitness level immediately prior to pregnancy confirmation.
Topic 01 — Pregnancy · Intensity Considerations

How Hard Is Too Hard?

Elite Athletes
Studies show elite athletes can manage varied training loads/intensities and still deliver healthy babies and return to sport
General Population
Most research is on moderate loads — moderate intensity ↑ blood sugar control, ↑ insulin usage, ↓ fatigue
Caution Zone
Vigorous exercise (incl. HIIT) tolerated well in healthy, previously-vigorous-trained females — but caution advised exceeding 90% max HR until more research exists

Topic 02 — Injury & Health Considerations

A Different Risk Profile

Female athletes face higher risk of specific injuries and health concerns than male athletes — possibly due to anatomical and biomechanical differences. Strength and conditioning professionals should emphasize lower extremity biomechanics and proper exercise technique.
Musculoskeletal
ACL · PFPS · Bone stress injuries
Neurological
Higher sport-related concussion rates
Systemic
Mental health · sleep · pelvic floor dysfunction

Topic 02 — ACL Injury · Prevention

Reducing ACL Injury Risk

When
Preparatory conditioning before puberty → optimizes training adaptations · continue across the lifespan
Mechanism
Most ACL injuries are noncontact — from deceleration, lateral pivoting, or landing
What Works
Progressive overload + plyometrics + agility + balance training, delivered by a S&C professional
Additional clinical trials are still needed to determine the best protocol for reducing ACL incidence and improving adherence in female athletes.
Topic 02 — Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome

PFPS — "Runner's Knee"

What It Is
Broad term for pain arising from the patellofemoral joint · a.k.a. runner's knee or jumper's knee
Female Risk
Females are 2×+ more likely to experience PFPS vs. male athletes
Root Cause
Attributed to hip mechanics before puberty · modified knee mechanics link to PFPS throughout development
S&C professionals should focus on lower extremity biomechanics, exercise technique, imbalances, and weaknesses in any athlete with PFPS.
Topic 02 — Bone Stress Injuries

Bone Stress Injuries & RED-S

Pattern
Stress fractures occur at higher rate in females vs. males · overuse injury from repetitive stress → abnormal bone remodeling
Watch For
Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) · monitor workload + bone health symptoms
Screening
Vitamin D levels · early sport specialization as risk factors
Continuing to prescribe osteogenically stimulating exercise and alerting workload through periodization may assist in reducing bone stress injuries.

Topic 02 — Applied Checklist

How Can Female Athletes Reduce Injury Risk?

  • Recommend preparticipation screening by a sports medicine physician, incl. risk-factor identification + musculoskeletal testing
  • Encourage year-round conditioning: resistance + plyometric + speed/agility + flexibility, periodized to each athlete
  • Ensure athletes learn and repeatedly demonstrate correct movement mechanics — jumping, landing, twisting, cutting
  • Precede every session with general + activity-specific dynamic warm-up targeting key muscle groups (e.g. posterior chain)
  • Provide augmented feedback within sessions to optimize skill transfer and biomechanics

Topic 02 — Sleep

Sleep & Injury Risk

The Problem
Most athletes get less-than-age-appropriate sleep · a known risk factor for ↑ injury risk
Female Athletes
Particularly likely to have low sleep duration (<7h/night) → daytime sleepiness + dysfunction; ↓ quantity/quality after training + competition
The Fix
Sleep hygiene education for athletes, coaches, and support staff → addresses barriers, improves sleep

Topic 02 — Applied Checklist

How Can Females Improve Health Outcomes?

  • Recommend working with appropriate medical staff for resources/services when signs of health concerns are identified
  • Encourage females to seek a mental health provider when initial symptoms of mental health disorders arise
  • Educate on appropriate sleep amount/quality and its role in recovery and injury risk reduction
  • Provide information on pelvic floor impairments and encourage a specialized physical therapist when signs/symptoms occur

Musculoskeletal, metabolic, and endocrine responses to resistance training in females.

Topic 03 — Musculoskeletal

Bone & Muscle Adaptations

Bone
Mechanical loading → skeletal remodeling → ↑ bone mass · higher-intensity RT → greater osteogenesis · preadolescence = opportune window
Hypertrophy
Short-term (up to 16 wks) relative CSA gains similar between sexes when measured with sophisticated techniques (e.g. CT)
Evidence
Female weightlifters/bodybuilders/track athletes (no steroids) show clear hypertrophy — though gains typically less than males; genetic disposition also a factor
Photo by Alora Griffiths on Unsplash
Topic 03 — Metabolic Adaptations

Anaerobic vs. Aerobic

Anaerobic Energy
Males: slightly ↑ proportion of type II (fast) fibers
Both sexes hypertrophy with anaerobic training
Males: greater absolute hypertrophy → ↑ PCr/glycogen storage, creatine kinase, glycolytic enzymes
Result: ↑ overall glycolytic capacity in males
Aerobic Energy
Females: ↑ proportion of type I (oxidative) fibers
More fatigue-resistant in prolonged events — despite lower VO₂max relative to body size
Oxidize more fat, less carb/protein during exercise
≈33% greater mitochondrial respiration than males (Cardinale, 2018)
These differences may present an advantage for females in most endurance activities, including ultraendurance events.
Topic 03 — Aerobic Energy · Cardiovascular

Cardiovascular Training Adaptations

Heart Size
More modest ↑ blood volume · similar/lesser ↑ in LV chamber size + wall thickness vs. males
Heart Function
Similar adaptations to males for LV function — including a greater ejection fraction
Running Economy
More efficient O₂ cost of running at various intensities · maintains running economy across a range of velocities
Females compensate for smaller hearts and lower O₂-carrying capacity through different mechanisms than males — not lesser aerobic performance.
Topic 03 — Endocrine · Testosterone

Testosterone in Females

Source
Much lower concentrations than males · produced in small amounts by ovaries + adrenal gland · resting levels highly variable between females
Acute Response
No significant elevation after an acute RT bout · with prolonged training, some acute rises appear — likely from ↓ rate of testosterone breakdown
Training Relevance
Relative hypertrophy/strength gains similar to males despite low testosterone · may still indicate individual trainability
Topic 03 — Endocrine · Growth Hormone

Growth Hormone & IGF-1

Resting GH
Higher at rest in females than males · with chronic training, resting GH is lower in females than males — may signal greater hormone sensitivity
Acute Response
Circulating GH rises 2–4× after an acute RT bout in females · rises similarly to males after chronic training
What Maximizes the GH Response
High total work (10RM)+ Large muscle groups+ Short rest (1 min)+ Multi-set protocols= ↑ Peak GH Response
Topic 03 — Endocrine · Cortisol

Cortisol Response

The Anticipatory Cascade
Anticipation of Exercise→ HPA Axis Activation→ Cortisol Rise
Trained vs. Untrained
Anticipatory rise greater in trained vs. sedentary females · response greatest with protocols producing high GH + blood lactate
vs. Males
Cortisol response to acute exercise is attenuated in females — likely from ↑ cortisol sensitivity or sex-hormone modulation of SNS/adrenal/HPA activity
Elite Females
Elite female athletes show no anticipatory rise, but higher cortisol secretion + different patterns overall
Estradiol
Progesterone
Topic 03 — Endocrine · Menstrual Cycle

The Menstrual Cycle

Two primary hormones released from the ovaries: estradiol and progesterone.
Menstrual bleeding begins the cycle → estradiol peaks and drops → progesterone rises + peaks in the second half, with a smaller second estradiol peak → both drop to their lowest by cycle's end.
Topic 03 — Endocrine · Estradiol

Estradiol: A Goldilocks Hormone

Low Estradiol
↑ muscle cell death · reduced skeletal muscle mass + strength
Normal Range
Protective effect on muscle · antioxidant + membrane stabilizer · promotes protein synthesis over degradation
High Estradiol
↓ power + performance · ↑ risk of catastrophic ligament injury
Because of estrogen's benefits to muscle, it's important for females to maintain normal menstrual cycle function to maximize training adaptations and reduce injury risk.
Topic 03 — Recap

Training Responses & Adaptations — Recap

MusculoskeletalRelative hypertrophy and bone response are similar to males; absolute magnitude is typically less.
MetabolicMales lean anaerobic/glycolytic; females lean aerobic/oxidative — a fatigue-resistance advantage in endurance.
EndocrineLower testosterone doesn't block strength gains; GH and cortisol responses follow female-specific patterns.
EstradiolNormal levels protect muscle — too little or too much both carry real performance and injury costs.

Topic 04 — Strength Differences

Absolute vs. Relative Strength

Absolute Strength (kg/N) Large Gap
Relative to Body Weight Gap Narrows
Relative to Fat-Free Mass Gap Disappears
Females have a similar number of muscle fibers as males, and strength is similar when normalized to muscle volume. Hypertrophy differences — not muscle quality — drive most of the absolute gap.
Topic 04 — Strength Differences

Upper vs. Lower Body

Lower Body
Absolute lower body strength is closer to male values than upper body
Relative to body weight: similar to males
Upper Body
Larger absolute gap vs. males than lower body
Relative to body weight: still somewhat less than males
Sex-related differences in body composition, anthropometrics, and fat-free mass distribution partly explain this pattern — visible in both recreationally trained individuals and elite athletes.

Topic 04 — Power Differences

Power Output

Lower Body Power
Similar between sexes when measured relative to lean body mass
Upper Body Power
Differences still present, even relative to lean mass
Possible Explanation
Sex differences in rate of force development (equivocal data) · females express lower % of type IIa myosin heavy chain isoform content
Topic 04 — Training-Induced Gains

Progressive Overload Works the Same Way

Volleyball
Traditional RT + weightlifting variations over ≈2.5 yrs → ↑ maximal strength + vertical jump in collegiate players
Basketball & Cycling
Plyometrics support female basketball players · velocity-based RT improves strength/power/hypertrophy in cyclists
Endurance Athletes
Adding heavy strength training → ↑ muscle strength, performance, size — without hurting running economy

With progressive overload and targeted training, female athletes experience substantial strength and power increases similar to male athletes.

Topic 04 — Menstrual Cycle & Performance

Does Cycle Phase Change Performance?

The Claim
Some individual studies suggest cycle phase affects performance in eumenorrheic females
Practical Barriers
Feasibility of tracking cycles across a team · variable use of hormonal contraceptives · menstrual cycle irregularities
Best Evidence
Most recent meta-analyses find minimal strength differences between phases · no systematic performance–phase relationship in trained athletes

Current evidence does not support modifying resistance training prescription based on a female athlete's menstrual cycle phase.

Resistance Training Considerations — Recap

ReframeAbsolute strength gaps shrink dramatically once normalized to body weight or fat-free mass.
Upper/LowerLower body strength tracks closer to male values than upper body, even relatively.

Chapter 08 — Closing

The Big Picture

Resistance exercise is a safe and effective training method for female athletes of all ages and abilities — improving performance, health markers, and psychosocial development while reducing injury risk.
Same Principles
Fundamental RT principles are the same for all athletes — sex differences change program details, not philosophy
Individual > Sex
Individual responses to resistance exercise vary greatly, independent of sex

Know the sex-related differences — then coach the individual athlete in front of you.
