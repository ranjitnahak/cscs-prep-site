Topic 01 — Foundation

Scalars
& Vectors

The language of Physics — and why direction changes everything in strength & conditioning

The Core Idea

Two Types of Quantities

Scalar
A quantity that only has a magnitude (size). Direction doesn't matter.
Think of it as Just a number. That's all you need.
Vector
A quantity that has both magnitude AND direction. You can't describe it without both.
Think of it as A number with a compass. Size + where it's pointing.
Real World Analogy

Think About Your Daily Run

"

"I ran 5 km today" → That's a scalar. Just the distance. No direction needed.

"I ran 5 km north" → That's a vector. Same distance, but now direction is part of the information.

In S&C, direction always matters — a force pushing straight down on a barbell is completely different from the same force applied at an angle.

In Your Training Room

Spot the Difference

  • Scalar Body weight: 80 kg — just a number, no direction
  • Vector Gravity pulling that athlete 80 kg downward — size + direction
  • Scalar Time of a 40m sprint: 4.8 seconds
  • Vector Velocity of that sprint: 8.3 m/s forward
  • Scalar Temperature in the gym: 28°C
  • Vector Force applied on a sled: 200 N at 30° angle

Three laws that govern every movement your athletes make — decoded for S&C

The Three Laws — At a Glance

Newton's Three Laws

Law 01
Law of Inertia
"An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion — unless acted upon by an external force."
Things don't change on their own. A force must make them.
Law 02
Law of Acceleration
"Force equals mass multiplied by acceleration."
More mass or more acceleration = more force required.
Law 03
Law of Reaction
"For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction."
Every force you apply gets pushed back with equal force.
Law 01 — Inertia
Things Don't Change on Their Own
"An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion — unless acted upon by an external force."
Ball at Rest
That ball on the grass stays there forever — until you kick it. No force, no change.
Ball in Motion
Once kicked, it keeps rolling — until friction or a wall stops it. Motion continues without force.
The first step of a sprint is the hardest — you're fighting inertia from rest. Decelerating an athlete demands just as much force as accelerating them.

Law 03 — Action & Reaction

Every Push Gets Pushed Back

The Law — Exactly as stated
"For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction."
Ball Example
When you throw a ball against a wall, the ball pushes the wall forward. The wall pushes the ball back with equal force. That's why it bounces.
Sprint Mechanics
When your athlete's foot pushes backward into the ground, the ground pushes forward on the foot with equal force. That's Ground Reaction Force — what propels them forward.
Lifting
When an athlete pushes down through their feet during a squat, the ground pushes back up — helping transfer force through the kinetic chain.
Ground Reaction Force
Every stride = action into ground → equal reaction forward
Key Takeaway — Topic 02

Newton's Laws in Every Session

Law 01
Inertia
The hardest part of any movement is overcoming the start. Force is needed to change any state of motion.
Law 02
F = ma
To move heavier athletes faster, you need more force. Strength training is force production training.
Law 03
Reaction
The ground pushes back. Ground reaction force is what moves athletes — teach them to use it.

Newton didn't write these laws for textbooks. They're happening on your training floor every single day.

Topic 03

Force

The push or pull that changes everything — what it is, how it's measured, and where it shows up in every training session

The Definition

What is Force?

Force is any push or pull that causes — or has the potential to cause — a change in the motion of an object.

No force → no change. Apply force → something moves, slows down, speeds up, or changes direction.

N
Force is measured in Newtons (N)
Named after — you guessed it — Newton
~10N
The force needed to hold a 1 kg weight against gravity. Simple reference point.
↕️
Force is a vector — it always has a magnitude AND a direction. (You already know this!)
Types of Force in S&C

Forces You Deal With Every Session

Gravity
9.81 m/s² downward.
Always on. Every rep fights it.
Muscle Force
Contracts → moves.
Every lift, sprint, jump.
Ground Reaction Force
Push down → ground pushes up.
Newton 3 in every stride.
Friction
Opposes motion.
Good for grip. Bad for sleds.
Applied Force
External, deliberate.
Bands, barbells, coaching cues.
Normal Force
Surface pushes back.
Perpendicular. Keeps you up.

Key Takeaway — Topic 03

Force is the Language of Movement

What Force Is
Any push or pull that changes or attempts to change the state of motion of an object. Always has magnitude and direction.
What Net Force Means
Multiple forces act simultaneously. Net force is what remains after they all cancel or add up. Net force determines the actual outcome of movement.
The Big Idea for S&C
Every exercise is a force equation. Muscle force vs gravity. Athlete push vs friction. Ground reaction vs body weight. When you understand force, you understand why every coaching cue exists.

You don't just train muscles. You train force production.

Your body is a system of levers. Understanding this changes how you see every exercise.

The Basics

What is a Lever?

Fulcrum
The fixed pivot point around which the lever rotates. It doesn't move — everything rotates around it.
In the body → Joint (e.g. elbow)
Effort
The force applied to the lever to move the load. This is your muscle producing force.
In the body → Muscle force
Load
The resistance being moved — the weight of the object or the body segment itself.
In the body → Dumbbell / limb weight
"

Think of a see-saw. The middle point it balances on is the fulcrum. One child pushing down is the effort. The other child is the load. Your elbow during a bicep curl works exactly the same way.

Lever & Fulcrum — Real Example
The Pushup is a Lever
Every pushup is a lever system in action.

Fulcrum — Toes on the ground (the pivot point)
Load — Body weight (what is being lifted)
Effort — Chest and tricep muscles pushing down into the floor
The longer the body, the longer the lever arm — and the harder it becomes. A full pushup is harder than a kneeling pushup for exactly this reason.
Topic 05

Lever
System

Three classes of levers — and why Class 3 dominates the human body

The Three Classes

Not All Levers Are The Same

Class 1
Fulcrum in the Middle
Effort — Fulcrum — Load
The fulcrum sits between the effort and the load. Classic see-saw arrangement. Body example: Head nodding — atlanto-occipital joint is the fulcrum, skull is the load, neck muscles are the effort.
Class 2
Load in the Middle
Effort — Load — Fulcrum
The load sits between effort and fulcrum. Mechanical advantage — less effort needed. Body example: Rising on tiptoes — toes are fulcrum, body weight is load, calf muscles are effort.
Class 3
Effort in the Middle
Load — Effort — Fulcrum
Effort sits between load and fulcrum. Requires more force but produces greater speed and range of motion. Most muscles in the body operate this way.

Class 3 Lever — Most Common in Body
Bicep Curl — Class 3
Fulcrum — Elbow joint
Effort — Bicep muscle, attaching close to the elbow
Load — Dumbbell, far end of the forearm

The muscle effort sits between the fulcrum and the load. Mechanically disadvantaged — the bicep must produce far more force than the dumbbell weighs.
This is why a 10 kg dumbbell curl demands much more than 10 kg of muscle force. Class 3 levers always demand more from the muscle.
Class 2 Lever — Load Between Effort and Fulcrum
Dumbbell Carry — Class 2
Fulcrum — Ball of the foot / ankle joint
Load — Body weight + dumbbell through centre of mass
Effort — Calf muscles pulling the heel up

The load sits between the fulcrum and the effort — mechanically advantaged, allowing the calf to support heavy loads with relatively less force.
Class 2 levers are rare in the body but powerful when present. The calf is one of the strongest muscles because of this mechanical advantage.
Topic 06

Torque

Force × Moment Arm. You already know both — now put them together.

Torque — Real World
Every Swing is Torque
Watch a batter connect with the ball. What you're seeing is torque — rotational force generated through the kinetic chain.

Force — Muscle contraction through legs, hips, core, arms
Moment Arm — Distance from the axis of rotation (spine/hip) to the bat
Result — Torque that transfers through the bat into the ball
The longer the bat and the further from the axis of rotation, the greater the moment arm — and the greater the torque produced at impact.
The Definition

What is Torque?

τ = F × d
τ

d
Moment Arm (metres)

Torque is rotational force — the tendency of a force to cause rotation around a joint.

A force applied far from the joint creates more torque than the same force applied close to the joint. That's why a long wrench loosens a bolt more easily than a short one — same force, more torque.

Torque in the Training Room

Where You See Torque Every Day

Deadlift — Hip Torque
↑ d
Bar drifts away from body → moment arm increases → hip torque spikes → lower back takes the load. Keep the bar close to minimise torque.
Bicep Curl — Elbow Torque
90°
Torque at the elbow is highest at 90° — when the forearm is horizontal. This is when the moment arm is longest. Most difficult point of the curl.
Plank — Shoulder Torque
↑↑
Extended arms create a huge moment arm at the shoulder. That's why a long-lever plank is so much harder — not more weight, just more torque.
"

When your athletes struggle at a specific point in a lift, they're often fighting peak torque at a joint — not just the weight on the bar. Understanding torque helps you identify exactly where and why.

Key Takeaway — Topic 06

Torque is Rotational Force

The Formula
τ = F × d

Torque goes up when force increases OR when the moment arm increases. You can change torque without changing the load.
The Connection
Force (Topic 3) × Moment Arm (Topic 4) = Torque (Topic 5).

Each topic built on the last. This is how physics stacks — not random facts, but a connected system.
The Coaching Insight
You don't need to add weight to make an exercise harder. Change the moment arm — move the load further from the joint — and torque increases. Same physics, completely different demand on the athlete.

Torque is why technique isn't just about form — it's about physics.
